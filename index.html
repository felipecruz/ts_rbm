01THE IDEA

From “what is this?”
to “what could this be?”

A model can learn to recognize a circle. But a generative model learns something different: which arrangements of pixels are likely to look like the patterns it has seen.

Once it learns those relationships, we can ask it to make another pattern. Not by handing it a drawing rule, but by letting it sample from the possibilities it has learned.

Our machine is a Restricted Boltzmann Machine, or RBM. It’s a compact generative model with just two layers. Small enough to inspect. Interesting enough to surprise us.

Start with 225 little yeses and nos.

Look at one of those drawings. Each square is either on or off. Read across the first row, then the next, and you get a list of 225 numbers. The drawing hasn’t changed. We’ve just given it a form the model can work with.

v means the whole picture. vᵢ means pixel number i. A 1 is a green square; a 0 is an empty one. That’s all the notation is saying.

We’ll write p(v) for the probability the model assigns to a complete drawing. Learning changes these probabilities. Generating explores the drawings they make possible.

↘READ THE TRACE

What is that falling line measuring?

During training, the machine tries to rebuild each example. Suppose a pixel is on, but the reconstruction gives it only an 80% chance of being on. The miss is 1 − 0.8 = 0.2. We square that miss, giving 0.2² = 0.04, so misses in opposite directions can’t cancel out.

The chart averages those squared misses over every pixel and every example. Written compactly, that is mean squared error:

ṽᵢ⁽ⁿ⁾
The reconstructed chance of that pixel being on.
Σ
“Add these up.” First the pixels, then the examples.
N

This is a reading of the reconstruction before each training update. A low value means familiar examples are being rebuilt closely. It doesn’t tell us whether generated samples are diverse or interesting. We’ll meet the actual learning rule below.

FIRST, THE INTUITION

Each connection is a small vote.

Imagine a hidden unit listening to the drawing. Each lit pixel contributes a vote through its connection. A positive weight pushes the unit toward “on”; a negative weight pushes it toward “off.” The unit also has a bias: a starting preference before the pixels vote.

The little weight map below makes those votes visible. Pick a hidden unit after training. You’re looking at its 225 connections, one for every square in the drawing. A unit may respond to a mixture of features; it doesn’t have to stand for a named shape.

NOW, WRITE DOWN THE IDEA

Add the votes. Turn the total into a chance.

To find the total evidence for one hidden unit, start with its bias and add the votes from the lit pixels. A pixel that’s off contributes zero. We’ll call the total zⱼ:

wᵢⱼ
The weight connecting pixel i to hidden unit j—one square in the weight map.
bⱼ
That hidden unit’s learned starting preference.

The total can be any number. A probability has to fit between 0 and 1. The sigmoid, written σ, makes that conversion smoothly: negative evidence gives a chance below one-half; positive evidence gives a chance above it. At the default randomness, we write:

A chance is not yet a decision. If the probability is 0.8, the model draws an on/off state like a coin weighted toward “on.” It can still come up “off.” That small act of sampling is where the variation comes from.

BACK TO THE TRAIN BUTTON

Keep the associations the examples support.

Suppose a pixel and a hidden unit tend to be active together in the examples. Their connection should grow stronger. But if the machine’s own reconstruction already produces that association too often, the update should push back.

So every training pass compares two averages: how strongly the pair appears together in the examples, and how strongly it appears in a short reconstruction. The difference tells us which way to adjust the connection.

Δwᵢⱼ
The small adjustment added to this connection’s weight.
η = 0.12
How big an adjustment to make: this experiment’s learning rate.
qⱼ, q̃ⱼ
The hidden unit’s on-probability for the original drawing and for its reconstruction.
⟨·⟩
Average over all training examples. The tilde marks a reconstructed quantity.

For example, an average association of 0.6 in the data and 0.4 in the rebuild gives Δw = 0.12(0.6 − 0.4) = 0.024. A small positive update: the next pass will give this connection a stronger vote.

This is our implementation’s one-step contrastive divergence, or CD-1. It samples the hidden states, rebuilds pixel probabilities, then recomputes the hidden probabilities. The chart reports the reconstruction miss; the weights change through these association updates. Biases are adjusted too. Training reference ↗

BACK TO THE ANIMATION

One tick is a complete conversation.

During generation, the learned connections stay fixed. We begin with random pixels, draw hidden states using their probabilities, then let those hidden states vote on new pixels. A new picture appears. We repeat the same round trip.

The return trip uses the same connections in the other direction. Each pixel adds up votes from the hidden units, plus its own bias aᵢ. If there are m hidden units, its on-probability at the default randomness is:

A pixel’s chance of being on is the sigmoid of its bias plus votes from the hidden units.

That repeated back-and-forth is Gibbs sampling. A frame can settle, wander, or revisit an earlier pattern. The 100-step playback lets us observe a short chain; it does not guarantee that the chain has fully explored the model’s distribution.

BACK TO THE RANDOMNESS SLIDER

The same evidence. A softer decision.

Turn up randomness and the machine becomes less decisive about the votes it receives. Turn it down and it follows those votes more strongly. The learned connections stay fixed; we change how their total is converted into a chance.

The slider’s value is a temperature, written T. Divide the evidence by it before applying the same sigmoid:

Hold the evidence at z = 2. Here is what the three slider settings mean for that same unit:

MORE DECISIVET = 0.598.2%chance of “on”
DEFAULTT = 188.1%chance of “on”
MORE VARIABLET = 1.579.1%chance of “on”

Higher temperature pulls probabilities toward 50%, whether the evidence is positive or negative. It can make samples more variable, but it doesn’t guarantee a more interesting result. Generate applies this setting to both layers. Restore uses T = 1.

04 / THE BIGGER PICTURE

A tiny model.
A surprisingly big idea.

This machine has no idea what a circle is. It has learned that some pixels tend to appear together. From those relationships, it can produce a pattern it wasn’t explicitly handed.

That is the generative idea: learn a world of possibilities, then draw something from it. With only a handful of examples, this little machine may repeat, blend, or lose a pattern. Those limits are part of what makes it worth exploring.
